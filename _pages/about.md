## Short Academic Background
I'm a postdoctoral researcher in Psychology at New York University (NYU), working with [Dr. Marjorie Rhodes](https://kidconcepts.org/about). I earned my PhD in Psychological Sciences (Developmental concentration) in 2024 from the University of Connecticut (UConn), where I worked with [Dr. Letty Naigles](https://cll.research.uconn/edu/) on the language abilities of autistic children. I also completed a [graduate certificate in Cognitive Sciences](https://cogsci.uconn.edu/graduate-certificate) at UConn.

Before beginning my doctoral training, I earned my MA in Psychology from The New School for Social Research (NSSR) in 2019, where I worked with [Dr. Joan Miller](https://www.nssrculturalpsychologylab.com/) studying cross-cultural differences in children's prosocial behavior as portrayed in picture books. I received my BS in Psychology from Juniata College in 2017, where I worked with Dr. Anne Gilman studying cross-cultural differences in students' preferences for visual versus verbal learning.

## Longer Background (aka the long-winded version)
I was born and raised in New York City to immigrant parents—my mother is from Taiwan and my father is from Malaysia. Neither of my parents attended college, so when it came time to navigate SATs, college applications, and financial aid, much of the process was new to my family and me. Fortunately, I attended [The Brooklyn Latin School (TBLS)](https://www.brooklynlatin.org/), a public high school with an International Baccalaureate (IB) curriculum. The combination of the IB program and a dedicated college-prep course pushed me to think seriously about what I wanted from college. I came away knowing that I wanted a small, close-knit academic community where I could explore the humanities and social sciences.

That search took me from New York City to Huntingdon, a small town in central Pennsylvania, where I attended [Juniata College](https://www.juniata.edu/). At Juniata, I explored psychology broadly, taking courses in developmental, cognitive, abnormal, and biological psychology. Over time, I became especially interested in questions about language: how the way we communicate reflects and potentially shape how we understand ourselves and other people. I also began conducting research with Anne Gilman on cross-cultural differences in students' preferences for visual versus verbal learning.

Because I entered college with credits from the IB program, I had the option to graduate in three years. I ultimately chose to do so for financial reasons. Although graduating early made college more affordable, it also meant that I had less time to build the kind of sustained research experience I wanted before applying to PhD programs. Rather than applying directly, I decided to pursue a master's degree and use that time to figure out what kinds of questions I most wanted to study.

That decision brought me back to NYC and to [NSSR](https://go.graduate.newschool.edu/psychology?utm_source=google&utm_medium=cpc&utm_campaign=1755_grad_on_campus_psychology&utm_content=masters&utm_segment=g&utm_term=graduate%20schools%20for%20psychology&eab_mrkt_id=159644778465&gad_source=1&gad_campaignid=21123914301&gbraid=0AAAAAqszkOTWq0pm03ho2j5R8kep0oX00&gclid=CjwKCAjww-3VBhAcEiwAwUUIuyBFD59RzFzN5FH2ysh7PsFrZ-9u4CXKaVsODoVtm2o769RbOFx4YBoCZ7UQAvD_BwE), where I joined Joan Miller's Cultural Psychology Lab. There, I became interested in how language and culture intersect with social behavior. My master's research examined cross-cultural differences in how children's prosocial behaviors are represented in picture books. More broadly, this work made me curious about how differences across languages and cultures can provide clues to the social practices and expectations that children encounter as they grow up.

By the time I began my PhD at the University of Connecticut, I knew that I was especially interested in the social uses of language, or pragmatics. One way to understand how communication works is to study contexts in which it may work differently, which drew me to research on neurodivergent development. Working with Letty Naigles in the Child Language Lab, I studied language and communication in autistic children. I became particularly interested in approaches that move beyond cataloging autistic children's presumed "deficits" and instead ask about variation in language profiles, communicative strengths, and the demands that different social contexts place on communication.

My graduate training also coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, which fundamentally changed how developmental research could be conducted. When in-person data collection became difficult and nearly impossible, I turned to existing language corpora and developed skills in corpus analysis, conversation analysis, and natural language processing. What began partly as a practical response to the pandemic became an important part of how I think about research: naturally occurring conversations contain an enormous amount of information about how people navigate social interactions in real time.

At the same time, my research interests began to broaden. Rather than asking only how children's language abilities differ, I became increasingly interested in what children accomplish through language. In particular, I wanted to understand how children use conversation to create and maintain social relationships. This became the focus of my dissertation, which examined how school-aged children coordinate their language with friends and how features of their conversations, such as linguistic alignment and the use of discourse markers and backchannels, relate to friendship quality.

I've now returned to NYC as a postdoc at NYU, where my work approaches the relationship between language and children's social worlds from another direction. In addition to asking how children use language to navigate relationships, I now ask how the language children hear shapes their developing beliefs about other people. Working with Marjorie Rhodes, I study how generic language can shape children's beliefs about social categories, particularly gender.

My postdoc work began as part of an [NSF-funded project](https://ecrhub.org/ecr-projects?id=2400839) examining whether changing the language adults use with children could reduce the development of rigid gender beliefs. Following the abrupt termination of that federal funding, we have continued the work with internal support. The project has also pushed me toward a question that increasingly ties together the different stages of my research: **How does language both reflect and create our social worlds?** Whether I'm studying conversations between friends, communication in neurodivergent populations, or the language adults use to describe social groups, I'm interested in how seemingly small choices in everyday language can shape how children understand and relate to the people around them.

## Outside Academia
I'm a first-generation Asian American and grew up speaking English (my native language) and Mandarin (my heritage language). Perhaps unsurprisingly for someone who studies language for a living, I also love learning new languages. Over the years, I've picked up bits and pieces of Spanish, Latin, Korean, and Japanese—though I make no claims of fluency!

Despite being born and raised in NYC, I'm happiest somewhere much quieter. I love being in the woods, spotting wildlife right outside my back door, and exploring new parks and trails with my husband and our dogs.

I also love cooking, especially for other people. I sometimes joke that if academia doesn't work out, I'll open a café or restaurant someday. When I'm not cooking (or working), you can usually find me reading true crime, murder mysteries, or historical fiction, listening to early 2000s R&B or K-pop, or binge-watching Law & Order: SVU.
