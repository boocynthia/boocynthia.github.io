<p class="page-subtitle">
  A plain-language guide to my research on language, learning, and social understanding.
</p>

I study how language shapes children’s social worlds: how they use language to connect with other people, and how the language they hear shapes what they come to believe about others.

My research brings together developmental psychology, linguistics, cognitive science, and computational methods. Across my work, I study both neurotypical and neurodivergent development—and I am interested in communication as something people build *together*.

---

## How do children adapt their language across social contexts?

Talking with a close friend feels different from answering a teacher’s question, telling a story, or meeting someone new. Even children adjust how they communicate depending on who they are with and what they are doing together.

I study the small but meaningful features of conversation that help people connect. These include words such as *yeah* and *mhm*, which show someone is listening; phrases such as *like* or *well*, which help organize what we say; and **linguistic alignment**, or the tendency for conversation partners to become a little more similar in how they speak.

By studying natural conversations between school-aged friends, I ask questions such as:

- When do children change the way they speak to fit a conversation?
- What does it mean when friends begin using similar words or conversational styles?
- Can the ways children communicate with one another tell us something about the quality of their friendship?

**Why it matters:** Conversation is more than sharing information. It is one way children build relationships, show attention, and feel understood.

## How does context shape neurodivergent communication?

People often talk about communication differences as though they are fixed qualities of an individual. My research asks a different question: **How do communication patterns change depending on the situation?**

I study autistic, ADHD, AuDHD (co-occurring diagnoses of autism and ADHD), and neurotypical children and adolescents across different kinds of interactions—for example, telling a story alone, talking with another person, or completing a socially demanding task.

The same person may communicate differently depending on:

- who they are talking with,
- whether the conversation is interactive,
- what they are being asked to do, and
- how socially or cognitively demanding the situation is.

For example, some differences between neurodivergent and neurotypical children’s language become smaller—or disappear—during certain interactive tasks. In other work, adolescents’ use of conversational words such as *um* differs when they are telling a story alone versus speaking with another person.

**Why it matters:** Communication is not something that happens in a vacuum. Understanding context helps us move away from one-size-fits-all ideas about what “good” communication looks like.

## How does language shape children’s beliefs about social groups?

Children learn about their social world not only from what adults explicitly teach them, but also from the subtle patterns in everyday language.

One focus of my research is **generic language**: broad statements about groups, such as “boys play with trucks” or “girls are good at reading.” These statements can sound ordinary, but children may treat them as clues that groups are naturally and permanently different.

I study questions such as:

- How do children interpret statements about whole groups?
- Does it make a difference to say “girls are good at math” rather than “this girl is good at math”?
- How do broad statements about gender influence what children remember later?
- How does the language children hear at home relate to their beliefs about gender?

In parent–child conversations, my colleagues and I found that parents’ use of broad statements about gender predicted children’s beliefs about gender six months later. I also study how people remember information about social groups, including when adults may unintentionally reconstruct information in ways that fit familiar stereotypes.

**Why it matters:** Everyday language can shape what children think is possible—for themselves, for other people, and for society.

## How do children develop concepts of neurodiversity?

Children are learning about neurodiversity—autism, ADHD, and other forms of human difference—from the people, language, and examples around them. Yet we still know relatively little about how they form these ideas.

My emerging research brings together my work on language, social learning, and neurodivergent communication. I ask how children come to understand neurodiversity, how they reason about similarities and differences among people, and how language can shape expectations about neurodivergent peers.

For example:

- What do children infer when they hear “autistic children like routines”?
- Does talking about a group in broad terms make children think its members are all alike?
- How do different explanations for neurodevelopmental differences influence children’s ideas about change, friendship, and inclusion?

I am interested not only in what children believe, but in how those beliefs are constructed through everyday social interactions.

**Why it matters:** The language adults use can help children understand neurodiversity with nuance, recognize individual variation, and approach differences with curiosity and respect.

## How I study these questions

No one method can capture the full richness of social communication. Depending on the question, my research may involve:

- playful experiments with children and adults;
- real or semi-structured conversations;
- detailed behavioral and language coding;
- online developmental research;
- natural language processing and other computational tools; and
- collaborations with students, families, educators, and other researchers.
