I study **how language shapes children's social worlds**. My research brings together developmental psychology, cognitive science, and linguistics to ask how children use language to navigate social interactions and how the language they hear shapes what they come to believe about other people.

Across these questions, I study both neurotypical and neurodivergent development using a combination of **experimental, naturalistic, and computational methods**.

---

## How do children adapt their language across social contexts?

Language is inherently social: how we communicate depends on who we're talking to, what we're doing together, and the relationship we have with our conversational partner. I study how children coordinate their language with other people and what these patterns of coordination can tell us about their developing social relationships.

Using naturally occurring conversations between school-aged friends, I examine linguistic behaviors such as **backchannels** (*mhm, yeah*), **discourse markers** (*like, well*), and **linguistic alignment** (i.e., the tendency for conversational partners to become more similar in how they communicate). My work shows that children flexibly adjust these strategies across interactional contexts and that some forms of linguistic coordination are associated with friendship quality.

## How does context shape neurodivergent communication?

Rather than asking only whether neurodivergent children communicate differently from their neurotypical peers, I ask **when, why, and under what interactional conditions communicative differences emerge**.

Across studies of autistic, ADHD, AuDHD, and neurotypical children and adolescents, I examine how structural and pragmatic features of language vary across discourse contexts, conversational partners, and interactional demands. This work suggests that communicative differences are not necessarily fixed characteristics of individual speakers; they can emerge, disappear, or change depending on the context in which communication occurs.

For example, I have found that differences in language complexity between neurodivergent and neurotypical children disappeared during a socially and cognitively demanding virtual-reality task. In other work, autistic and non-autistic adolescents differed in their use of discourse markers during monologic storytelling but not during interactive conversations. Together, these findings motivate my broader interest in communication as something that is **jointly constructed between conversational partners**.

## How does language shape children's beliefs about social groups?

Children learn about their social world not only through what people explicitly teach them, but also through subtle features of everyday language. I study how these linguistic cues shape children's developing beliefs about **gender and other social groups**.

Much of my work in this area focuses on **generic language**—broad statements about categories, such as *boys play with trucks*. I examine how children interpret these statements, how exposure to them relates to essentialist beliefs about social categories, and how generic language affects the way social information is encoded and reconstructed in memory.

In naturalistic parent-child conversations, for example, we found that parents' use of generic statements about gender predicted children's gender essentialist beliefs approximately six months later. Other work examines what happens after generic information is encoded in memory: adults are particularly likely to misremember counter-stereotypical generic statements in stereotype-consistent ways, whereas children show better memory for information presented generically without yet demonstrating the same stereotype-consistent distortions.

## How do children develop concepts of neurodiversity?

My emerging work brings together my research on **neurodivergent communication** and my research on **language and social learning**. I am interested in how children develop concepts of autism and other forms of neurodiversity, how language shapes those representations, and how children's beliefs influence their expectations about and interactions with neurodivergent peers.

For example, does hearing a generic statement such as *autistic children like routines* encourage children to view autistic people as more similar to one another? How do explanations for neurodevelopmental differences shape children's beliefs about whether characteristics are stable or variable? And how do these beliefs relate to children's expectations about friendship, acceptance, and inclusion?

I plan to combine controlled experiments with naturalistic investigations of how caregivers and educators talk with children about neurodiversity. Ultimately, this work asks not only **what children believe about neurodiversity, but how those beliefs are constructed through language and social interaction**.

## Methods

My research combines methods from **developmental psychology, cognitive science, linguistics, and computational social science**. Depending on the research question, my work includes:

- Experimental studies with children and adults
- Naturalistic and semi-structured conversations
- Behavioral and discourse coding
- Corpus linguistics
- Natural language processing
- Computational analyses of conversational alignment
- Online developmental research

## Mentoring & Collaborative Research

Mentorship is central to how I approach research. I involve undergraduate and graduate researchers throughout the research process, from developing questions and designing studies to coding data, conducting analyses, and communicating findings.

I have directly mentored undergraduate honors theses, independent research projects, and graduate research. I encourage students to move from contributing to an existing project toward developing questions of their own and taking increasing intellectual ownership of the research.

Several of my mentees have presented their work at national conferences, and I aim to create research environments where **prior research experience is not a prerequisite for meaningful participation**. My goal is for students to develop both methodological skills and the confidence to ask and pursue their own research questions.
